The blind spot

A generation is losing muscle on purpose-built drugs. Nobody is measuring it.

GLP-1 medications work. They also take lean mass along with the fat — and the standard mitigation, “eat more protein,” is the most given and least verified instruction in medicine.

Protein adequacy is assessed today by asking people what they ate. Body composition confirms the loss months after it happened. Neither tells a patient, a clinician, or a drugmaker what is happening this week, while there is still time to act.

The same blind spot sits underneath healthy aging, recovery and performance. It has persisted for exactly one reason: amino acid status has never been practically observable outside a lab.

What it unlocks

Real-time visibility changes what people can decide.

Not a new number for its own sake. A signal that arrives early enough to change the outcome — for the person taking the drug, the clinician managing them, and the company that made it.

Protein adequacy becomes a number.

The most-given instruction in metabolic health finally becomes something anyone can check.

Muscle loss becomes preventable.

Body composition is a lagging indicator. Catching it while it is still correctable is a different product entirely.

Nutrition becomes individual.

Requirements move with age, medication, illness and training load. A personal signal replaces a population average.

Interventions become closed-loop.

Any protocol built around protein can be tuned against a response instead of a schedule.
